# Podcast Script — "The CNPD – Your Data Protection Rights"

**Part of the Biergerpakt programme**
**Hosts:** Anna (woman) and Tom (man)
**Length:** about 13 minutes — spoken slowly, in simple English

---

**ANNA:** Hello, and welcome! It's really nice to have you with us today.
**TOM:** Hello everyone!
**ANNA:** This podcast is part of the Biergerpakt. That's a programme of living together in Luxembourg, organized by the Ministry of Family Affairs, Solidarity, Living Together and Reception of Refugees. The idea is to help residents, and also cross-border workers, to discover Luxembourg — or maybe re-discover Luxembourg — and to meet new people.
**TOM:** Think about an ordinary day. You use a loyalty card in a shop, you order something online, you open an app on your phone, and you fill in a form at the doctor's.
**ANNA:** And each time, you leave a little information about yourself: your name, your address, what you buy, and sometimes even something about your health.
**TOM:** All of this is personal data. And in Luxembourg, there is an authority that checks how such data is used: the CNPD.
**ANNA:** In English, it is called the National Commission for Data Protection. The four letters come from its French name: Commission nationale pour la protection des données.
**TOM:** Today, we look at the rights that you have over your own data, and at what you can do when they are not respected. So what kind of organisation is the CNPD?
**ANNA:** It is an independent public institution. It checks that personal data is processed in a lawful way, and it protects the freedoms and fundamental rights of people when it comes to data protection and privacy.
**TOM:** And which rules does the CNPD supervise in its daily work?
**ANNA:** The best known one is a European regulation, the General Data Protection Regulation. Most people simply say GDPR. It has applied directly in all Member States of the European Union since 25 May 2018.
**TOM:** So people who live in Luxembourg and people who cross the border to work here are protected by the same regulation.
**ANNA:** Yes, because the regulation is the same in all of these countries. In Luxembourg, there are also national laws, for example a law of 1 August 2018 that organises the CNPD.
**TOM:** Is there anything that the CNPD does not supervise?
**ANNA:** Yes, there is one clear limit. It does not supervise the data processing of the courts when they do their work as judges, and that includes the public prosecutor's office.
**TOM:** Before we come to the rights, here is one expression that you will read again and again on the website: the data controller. That is simply the organisation that processes your data, or on whose behalf it is processed. It can be a company or a public body.
**ANNA:** So what can I ask from such a data controller?
**TOM:** The website of the CNPD presents your rights one by one. The first is the right to information. When an organisation collects your data, it must tell you who it is, why it needs the data, and how long it will keep it.
**ANNA:** Does it have to say this in legal language that nobody understands?
**TOM:** No, it is the opposite. The information must be easy to find, easy to understand, and written in clear and simple words.
**ANNA:** And what if the organisation did not get the data from me, but from somebody else?
**TOM:** Then it must still inform you within a reasonable time, and as a rule within one month at the latest.
**ANNA:** The second right is the right of access, and it is a very practical one. You can ask an organisation whether it holds data about you. If it does, you can get a copy of this data, and an explanation of how it is used.
**TOM:** Which details does the organisation have to give me?
**ANNA:** For example, which kinds of data it has, why it uses them, how long it keeps them, and who has received them. If the data did not come from you, it must also say where it comes from.
**TOM:** Do I have to pay to receive this copy?
**ANNA:** In principle, it is free. An organisation may only ask for a fee, or refuse, when requests are clearly unfounded or excessive, for example when someone repeats the same request again and again.
**TOM:** And how long can the organisation take to answer?
**ANNA:** It must answer within one month. If the request is complex, it can take up to two more months, but then it has to tell you why.
**TOM:** Once you have seen your data, you may notice a mistake. That is where the right to rectification comes in. If data about you is wrong, incomplete or out of date, you can ask for a correction.
**ANNA:** Can I also ask for my data to be deleted completely?
**TOM:** Yes, that is the right to erasure, which many people call the right to be forgotten. You can use it when there is no longer a reason to process your data.
**ANNA:** Could you give us one or two examples?
**TOM:** The website gives several. You close your account on a fitness app, so the data is no longer needed. Or you unsubscribe from a newsletter, so you take back your consent.
**ANNA:** I suppose that this right also has its limits.
**TOM:** Yes, it has limits, because an organisation can keep data when a law obliges it to do so, when the data is needed for freedom of expression and information, or when it is needed to defend a legal claim.
**ANNA:** There is a related right with a difficult name: de-referencing. It is about search engines. You can ask a search engine to remove certain results that appear when someone types your first name and your surname.
**TOM:** Does that also delete the original page on the internet?
**ANNA:** No, the content stays on the website that published it, and only the link in the search results disappears. This right is not absolute either, because it is weighed against freedom of expression and information.
**TOM:** Then there is the right to object. You can say no to a certain use of your data, for reasons linked to your particular situation, and you explain these reasons to the organisation.
**ANNA:** What about advertising, for example a newsletter from an online shop?
**TOM:** For advertising, it is even easier. You can object to the use of your data for commercial prospecting at any time, and you do not have to give any reason.
**ANNA:** Sometimes I do not want my data to be deleted, but I do not want it to be used either. Is there something in between?
**TOM:** Yes, that is the right to restriction of processing. Imagine that you say your data is wrong. While the organisation checks this, it may not use the data.
**ANNA:** Two rights are left, and they sound a little more technical. The first is data portability. You can receive the data that you gave to an organisation in a common format that a computer can read, and you can have it sent to another organisation.
**TOM:** That sounds useful when I move from one provider to another.
**ANNA:** That is the idea, and it applies to data that is processed by automated means, on the basis of your consent or of a contract.
**TOM:** And what is the last right on the list about?
**ANNA:** It is about decisions that are taken only by a computer, without any human being. The website gives the example of a bank that refuses a loan because the system said no.
**TOM:** What can I do in that situation?
**ANNA:** You can ask for an explanation of the logic behind the decision, you can give your point of view, and if necessary you can contest the decision.
**TOM:** Now comes the practical question: how do you use these rights? The first step is always the same. You contact the organisation itself, the data controller.
**ANNA:** Where do I find the right contact address?
**TOM:** Usually on the website of the organisation, in its privacy notice or data protection notice. Some organisations also have a special form, or a personal space where you can see your data directly.
**ANNA:** Is it enough to make a phone call?
**TOM:** It is better to make your request in writing, by letter, by email or with the form of the organisation. The CNPD also offers model letters on its website, for example for the right of access.
**ANNA:** Do I have to send a copy of my identity card with my request?
**TOM:** No, not automatically, because the organisation may ask for proof of identity only if it has reasonable doubts about who you are. Often, your email address or your customer number is enough.
**ANNA:** Now imagine that the deadline passes and nothing happens. Or you receive an answer, and you are not satisfied with it. Then you can lodge a complaint with the CNPD.
**TOM:** And how do you lodge such a complaint?
**ANNA:** The CNPD recommends its online complaint form, which you find on its website. You can also print the form, fill it in by hand and send it by post.
**TOM:** What should I add to the form?
**ANNA:** You attach the documents that show what you have already done, for example your letter to the organisation and its answer.
**TOM:** Once your complaint has arrived, the CNPD first checks whether it is justified. If the rules were broken, it first tries to put the situation right.
**ANNA:** And what if the organisation still does not follow the rules?
**TOM:** Then the CNPD has real powers. It can order an organisation to answer your request, to correct or erase data, or to stop a processing. It can also impose administrative fines.
**ANNA:** Can it also carry out checks inside an organisation?
**TOM:** Yes, it has investigative powers. It can demand information, carry out data protection audits, and enter the premises of an organisation, according to the procedures set by law.
**ANNA:** Will I be told what happens with my complaint?
**TOM:** Yes, one of its duties is to keep the person who complained informed about the progress and the outcome.
**ANNA:** Some data is very sensitive, for example data held by the police. Do I have rights there too?
**TOM:** Yes, you do, but under a separate law with specific rules. You can ask for access to data processed by the Police or by the Customs and Excise Administration, for example. This access can be limited, for instance to protect an investigation.
**ANNA:** And what can I do if my request is refused there?
**TOM:** Then you can exercise your rights indirectly, through the CNPD. It carries out the checks with the authority concerned, and it informs you at least that these checks took place.
**ANNA:** One more question about complaints. Can the CNPD award me money as compensation?
**TOM:** No, that is not its role, because to claim damages you have to go to court. The CNPD suggests that you consult a lawyer for this. You can also go to court if you are not satisfied with what the CNPD did.
**ANNA:** The CNPD does much more than handle complaints. It raises public awareness about the risks, the rules and the rights linked to personal data, with special attention to children.
**TOM:** Does it also advise the people who make the laws?
**ANNA:** Yes, it advises the Chamber of Deputies, the Government and other institutions, and you can read its opinions on its website.
**TOM:** And does it work together with other countries?
**ANNA:** Yes, it cooperates with the data protection authorities of the other Member States, and it contributes to the European Data Protection Board, so that the rules are applied in the same way everywhere.
**TOM:** Can I simply ask the CNPD a question, without making a complaint?
**ANNA:** Yes, you can, because informing people about their rights is one of its duties. There is an online form for individuals on the website, you can write a letter, and you can phone during the telephone hours shown on the contact page.
**TOM:** The website itself is cnpd dot l-u, and it exists in French, English and German. In the section for individuals, you find all the rights we talked about, the complaint form and the model letters.
**ANNA:** And is there something for organisations too?
**TOM:** Yes, there is a section for professionals, because organisations that process data have obligations. There are also thematic dossiers, news and publications.
**ANNA:** Time to sum up the most important points. First: the GDPR gives you rights that let you control how your personal data is used.
**TOM:** Second: you can ask for information, for access and a copy, for a correction and for erasure, and you can object, especially to advertising.
**ANNA:** Third: always contact the organisation first, preferably in writing. It normally has one month to answer, and in principle it costs you nothing.
**TOM:** Fourth: if that does not work, you can lodge a complaint with the CNPD, the National Commission for Data Protection, with the form on its website.
**ANNA:** You find all the information on cnpd dot l-u.
**TOM:** Your data says a lot about you, so it is good to know who uses it, and why.
**ANNA:** And now you know where to ask. That was our episode about the CNPD and your data protection rights. **ANNA:** Thank you so much for listening.
