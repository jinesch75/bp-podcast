# Podcast Script — "INLL – Learning Luxembourgish and Other Languages"

**Part of the Biergerpakt programme**
**Hosts:** Anna (woman) and Tom (man)
**Length:** about 11 minutes — spoken slowly, in simple English

---

**ANNA:** Hello, and welcome! It's really nice to have you with us today.
**TOM:** Hello everyone!
**ANNA:** This podcast is part of the Biergerpakt. That's a programme of living together in Luxembourg, organized by the Ministry of Family Affairs, Solidarity, Living Together and Reception of Refugees. The idea is to help residents, and also cross-border workers, to discover Luxembourg — or maybe re-discover Luxembourg — and to meet new people.
**TOM:** Imagine that you have just moved to Luxembourg. At the bakery, at work or in front of the school, people switch from one language to another all day long, and you would love to join in, maybe even in Luxembourgish.
**ANNA:** Many listeners know that feeling, and there is a public institute that helps with exactly this: the National Institute of Languages Luxembourg, or INLL for short. It is our topic today, with one big question: how can adults learn Luxembourgish?
**TOM:** Let's start with the basics, then: what kind of place is the INLL, and who is behind it?
**ANNA:** The INLL is a public centre where adults learn languages and can have their language skills certified. It comes under the Ministry of Education, Children and Youth. Its aims are to promote multilingualism, to help people find work and take part in society, and to bring people together through languages.
**TOM:** That fits this country very well: Luxembourg has three administrative languages, many people from abroad, and many cross-border workers, and the INLL takes this into account in the way it teaches.
**ANNA:** Which languages can you actually learn there? Only the languages of the country, or others too?
**TOM:** The choice is much wider than that: more than ten languages. Of course Luxembourgish, French and German, but also English, Portuguese, Spanish, Italian and Dutch, and even Russian, Arabic, Chinese, Japanese and Korean.
**ANNA:** That is quite a list. And where do the courses take place? Does everybody have to travel to Luxembourg City?
**TOM:** Not necessarily, because the INLL has three sites. The biggest one is in Luxembourg City, and there are two more: one in Belval, in the south of the country, and one in Mersch, in the centre. Not every language is taught at every site, but Luxembourgish, French, German and English are taught at all three.
**ANNA:** And who are the courses for: can a teenager come along with their parents?
**TOM:** No, the courses are for adults only. And here is something that surprises many people: every course is taught in the language you are learning, from the very first lesson, even for complete beginners.
**ANNA:** Straight into the new language, then. That can feel a little scary at first, but it is also a good way to get used to it quickly. How are the levels organised?
**TOM:** The INLL follows the European scale for languages, which goes from A1 for beginners up to C2 for people who master a language. Most courses last one semester: one semester starts in autumn, in September, and the other one in spring, in February.
**ANNA:** Let's talk about signing up, because that is where many people hesitate. How do I get a place in a course?
**TOM:** Everything goes through the online portal MyINL. You create an account there, and you need a recommended level, because that level is what lets you enrol in a course. Complete beginners can simply choose the first level, A1, themselves.
**ANNA:** And if I already speak a little of the language, how does the INLL find out my level?
**TOM:** Then you first take a short test online, and after that you book an orientation talk with a teacher at the INLL, who gives you your recommended level. These talks are possible all through the school year. Once you have your level, you choose your course, as long as there are places left, and you pay within the deadline.
**ANNA:** What about the price of a course: does everybody pay the same?
**TOM:** The fee of each course is shown on MyINL, and books are not included. Some people pay a much lower fee, for example jobseekers sent to a course by ADEM, or people who signed the reception and integration contract. They need a voucher or a certificate from the service concerned.
**ANNA:** Now we come to the heart of our topic: Luxembourgish. What can somebody who wants to learn it find at the INLL?
**TOM:** There are many ways to learn it: classic courses have two or three lessons a week, but you can also learn online by video call, or combine lessons on site with work online. In hybrid courses, you even decide for each lesson whether you come in or join from home.
**ANNA:** One format is quite new, and it is called Moving Languages. It is an intensive course in Luxembourgish or in French that lasts eight weeks, with three hours of lessons a day. You learn with movement and with all five senses, and only in the new language, from the first day.
**TOM:** Eight weeks sounds short: what can you really reach in that time?
**ANNA:** The goal is the level A2 in speaking, for people who start with no knowledge at all. In other words, after two months you can manage simple everyday conversations. The method comes from Liechtenstein, and the INLL developed it in Luxembourg together with the Adult Education Service.
**TOM:** And what about people who have little time and would rather learn at their own pace? Is there something for them too?
**ANNA:** Yes: there is a guided self-study course in Luxembourgish. You work mostly on your own, on an online platform, with a few live video sessions and a weekly online support session. At the end, you take a speaking test on site, in Luxembourg City.
**TOM:** And between lessons, the INLL even has its own podcast, in simple Luxembourgish for beginners. On site, learners also find a media library, language tandems, and events where people meet and talk in the languages they are learning.
**ANNA:** How do I know whether I am making progress: is there a test at the end of a course?
**TOM:** In most semester courses, your work is assessed along the way, with a speaking test at the end, and you need to pass to move up to the next level. You then get a skills assessment that shows your level, and you can download it from MyINL, together with a certificate of attendance.
**ANNA:** That brings us to the test that many listeners have heard of: the Sproochentest. It is the Luxembourgish language test for nationality, and the INLL organises it. Many people who apply for Luxembourg nationality have to pass it, and it is the only certificate that counts for that application.
**TOM:** What happens in the test itself: is there a long text to write?
**ANNA:** No, there is no essay: the test has two parts, on the same half day. First, speaking, at level A2: for about ten minutes, you talk with an examiner about an everyday topic, such as work, family or hobbies, and you describe a picture. Second, listening, at level B1: you hear three recordings, for example a radio message and a conversation, and you answer questions on a tablet.
**TOM:** And how do you pass: do the two parts count the same?
**ANNA:** Speaking counts the most: you pass if you get at least half of the points there. If your speaking mark is a little lower, a good listening result can make up for it, as long as the average of both parts reaches half of the points.
**TOM:** That sounds fair, so where does the test take place, and how do you sign up?
**ANNA:** It takes place in Luxembourg City and in Belval, and you sign up through MyINL. Demand is very high, so it is wise to book early. If you do not come and have no valid reason, the fee is not refunded. And if you have a disability, you can ask for adapted conditions, with a medical document.
**TOM:** And if it does not work out the first time, can you try again?
**ANNA:** Yes, as often as you need: you can register again once you have received your previous result. Once you pass, your certificate does not expire. And on the website, you find example tests to prepare at home.
**TOM:** Good to know, and one thing the INLL does not offer is the citizenship courses for nationality: these are organised by the Adult Education Service and its partners.
**ANNA:** Besides the Sproochentest, are there other certificates in Luxembourgish that you can take?
**TOM:** Yes, there are two more: the first is a national diploma for Luxembourgish as a foreign language, from level A2 to C1, which can be useful at work, and you do not need to have followed a course at the INLL to take it. The second is a short test of Luxembourgish spelling. And for other languages, the INLL is an exam centre for well-known international certificates, such as the Cambridge exams for English or the Goethe certificate for German.
**ANNA:** The INLL does not only teach learners: it also trains teachers. There is a training for people who want to teach Luxembourgish to adults, one for teaching languages for work, and one to become a communication coach: a colleague who helps others to speak Luxembourgish in a team with many nationalities.
**TOM:** And one last tip: if you work in Luxembourg, there is a special language leave to learn Luxembourgish, and our episode about lifelong learning explains it. The website of the institute is inll dot l-u, in four languages, with the questions people ask most often and a contact form.
**ANNA:** Before we finish, what are the main points to keep in mind from this episode?
**TOM:** First, the INLL is a public institute where adults can learn more than ten languages, including Luxembourgish, at three sites, in class, online or at their own pace. Second, you sign up through MyINL, with a recommended level: beginners choose A1, the others take a short test and an orientation talk.
**ANNA:** And third, the Sproochentest, the Luxembourgish test for nationality, has a speaking part and a listening part, and you can take it again as often as you need. Because every new language opens a door to new people. That was our episode about the INLL, the National Institute of Languages Luxembourg. This podcast is part of the Biergerpakt, a programme that favours living together in Luxembourg: discovering the country, and meeting new people. If you want to discover more activities offered by the Biergerpakt, such as guided visits, online information sessions, a language learning mobile application, creative workshops or training sessions, simply head over to biergerpakt.lu where you will find detailed information. It is open to all residents, luxembourgish and non-luxembourgish, as well as to cross-border workers. And the activities are free of charge. You simply need to join the Biergerpakt for free at biergerpakt.lu.
**TOM:** Thank you, everyone. Take care, and see you next time!
**ANNA:** Thank you so much for listening.
