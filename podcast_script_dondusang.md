# Podcast Script — "Giving Blood in Luxembourg"

**Part of the Biergerpakt programme**
**Hosts:** Anna (woman) and Tom (man)
**Length:** about 10 minutes — spoken slowly, in simple English

---

**ANNA:** Hello, and welcome! It's really nice to have you with us today.
**TOM:** Hello everyone!
**ANNA:** This podcast is part of the Biergerpakt. That's a programme of living together in Luxembourg, organized by the Ministry of Family Affairs, Solidarity, Living Together and Reception of Refugees. The idea is to help residents, and also cross-border workers, to discover Luxembourg — or maybe re-discover Luxembourg — and to meet new people.
**TOM:** I have a question to start with, Anna. Do you know your blood group?
**ANNA:** I do, actually. It's written on my blood donor card, which I received the second time I gave blood.
**TOM:** Then you're the right person for today's topic: blood donation in Luxembourg, and the website dondusang dot l-u.
**ANNA:** "Don du sang" is French, and it simply means "blood donation". The website exists in French, German and English.
**TOM:** And who is behind this website?
**ANNA:** The Luxembourg Red Cross. It is the only organisation in Luxembourg that collects blood and prepares it for the hospitals. Since July 2026, this work is carried by a Red Cross foundation dedicated to blood transfusion.
**TOM:** Does that change anything for the donors?
**ANNA:** No, nothing changes for them: the same places, the same teams, and the same website.
**TOM:** Let's start with the big question. Why is giving blood so important?
**ANNA:** Because blood cannot be made in a factory. Researchers are working on it, but today, the only source is another human being.
**TOM:** And when do hospitals need blood?
**ANNA:** After a serious accident, during an operation, or when a mother loses a lot of blood while giving birth. And also for people whose body cannot make enough blood, for example during a cancer treatment.
**TOM:** How much blood does Luxembourg need?
**ANNA:** The website gives a simple number: about one hundred bags of blood have to be collected every weekday, to cover the needs of the hospitals.
**TOM:** And is there enough blood in Luxembourg?
**ANNA:** The Red Cross answers "yes and no". Yes, because so far the hospitals have always received what they needed. No, because the reserves are often close to their lower limit.
**TOM:** And blood doesn't keep forever, does it?
**ANNA:** No, and that's the point. A donation is separated into its parts. Red blood cells can be kept for about six weeks. Platelets, which help to stop bleeding, keep for only about one week.
**TOM:** So the hospitals need new donations all the time, and not only after an appeal in the news.
**ANNA:** Exactly, and that's why regular donors are so precious.
**TOM:** Now, who can give blood?
**ANNA:** The basic rule is simple: you are in good health, you are at least 18 years old, and you weigh more than 50 kilos.
**TOM:** Is there an upper age limit?
**ANNA:** Yes, there is. The first donation has to take place before the age of 60, and the last one at 65. A doctor of the transfusion centre can sometimes allow an exception.
**TOM:** I've heard that sometimes you have to wait before you can give. Is that true?
**ANNA:** Yes, that's true. In some situations, you have to wait for a while. After a new tattoo or a piercing, for example, it's four months.
**TOM:** And what about after a trip abroad?
**ANNA:** It depends on the destination. After a trip to a tropical region, you wait six months. For some other destinations, it's about one month.
**TOM:** And what if I've simply had a cold?
**ANNA:** Then you wait two weeks after you feel well again. And after antibiotics, you wait one month after the end of the treatment.
**TOM:** That's a lot to remember.
**ANNA:** You don't have to remember it. The website has a page with all these waiting times, and also a short online test. In a few minutes, you see whether you can probably donate.
**TOM:** Why do you say "probably"?
**ANNA:** Because the test is only a first indication. The real decision is made on the day itself, in a short conversation with a health professional.
**TOM:** Let's go through a donation step by step. What is the first step?
**ANNA:** You make an appointment on dondusang dot l-u. You choose the place and the date. The booking runs through Doctena, the platform that many people already use for their doctor's appointments.
**TOM:** Is an appointment compulsory?
**ANNA:** No, it isn't. An appointment is recommended, because you wait less, but you can also come without one.
**TOM:** And what do I do before I go?
**ANNA:** You fill in a medical questionnaire, the day before or on the day itself. It asks about your health, operations, travels and risky situations.
**TOM:** Those are quite personal questions.
**ANNA:** They are, and they protect two people: you, and the patient who will receive your blood. So it's important to answer honestly. And the conversation that follows is confidential.
**TOM:** Should I come with an empty stomach, like for a blood test at the laboratory?
**ANNA:** No, please don't! Eat normally and drink enough water. And avoid intense sport just before and just after the donation.
**TOM:** And what do I need to bring with me?
**ANNA:** An identity document. That's all you need the first time.
**TOM:** And then comes the needle. Be honest with me: does it hurt?
**ANNA:** You feel a small prick at the start, like for a normal blood test. After that, you just lie back and relax.
**TOM:** How long does it all take?
**ANNA:** The donation itself takes about ten minutes. With the reception, the conversation and the rest afterwards, plan about one hour.
**TOM:** And how much blood is taken?
**ANNA:** A little less than half a litre. For a healthy adult, that is safe. The body replaces the liquid very quickly, and the blood cells within a few weeks.
**TOM:** Can I catch a disease when I give blood?
**ANNA:** No, you can't. The needle and the bag are sterile, and they are used only once.
**TOM:** And afterwards, there's something to eat, I hope.
**ANNA:** Of course there is. You stay for a little while in the rest area, with a free drink and a snack, and the team checks that you feel well.
**TOM:** What happens to my blood after that?
**ANNA:** It is tested, among other things for infections like hepatitis and HIV. If something unusual is found, you are contacted. If you hear nothing, everything is fine.
**TOM:** And when do I get the donor card you mentioned?
**ANNA:** You receive it at your second visit, and it shows your blood group.
**TOM:** How often can a person give blood?
**ANNA:** Men can give every three months, and women every four months.
**TOM:** On the website, I also saw the word "plasma". What is that exactly?
**ANNA:** Plasma is the liquid part of the blood. It is yellow, and it makes up about half of our blood.
**TOM:** And is it possible to give only plasma?
**ANNA:** Yes, it is. A machine separates the plasma, and your blood cells are given back to you. That's why you can give plasma more often: once a month.
**TOM:** What is plasma used for?
**ANNA:** Mostly to make medicines for people with rare diseases or with a weak immune system. It also helps people with severe burns.
**TOM:** Does a plasma donation take longer?
**ANNA:** Yes, plan up to two hours, especially the first time. And it is only possible at the centre in Luxembourg City.
**TOM:** That's a good moment to ask: where can people give blood?
**ANNA:** There are three possibilities. The first one is the Blood Transfusion Centre in Luxembourg City, on boulevard Joseph II. It is open from Monday to Friday.
**TOM:** And what about the south of the country?
**ANNA:** In Esch-Belval, you can give blood on Mondays and Tuesdays. And from Wednesday to Friday, a mobile team travels around the country, for example to Ettelbruck, Wiltz, Echternach or Grevenmacher.
**TOM:** And all the dates are on the website, I imagine.
**ANNA:** Yes, in a calendar. And on the home page, there is a "blood group barometer". It shows which blood groups are needed most at the moment.
**TOM:** One more important thing: giving blood is voluntary, and nobody is paid for it.
**ANNA:** That's right. It is a gift, from one person to another person they will never meet.
**TOM:** And what if someone would like to help, but cannot give blood?
**ANNA:** There are other ways to help. You can become a "blood donation ambassador": you talk about it with your friends, family and colleagues, and you share the appeals when the reserves are low.
**TOM:** You can also become a volunteer, for example to look after the donors during their break.
**ANNA:** And a company, a school or an association can organise a visit to the centre, or even host a blood collection.
**TOM:** That fits well with the Biergerpakt. Giving blood is a very concrete way of taking part in life in Luxembourg.
**ANNA:** Let's bring it together. What should people remember?
**TOM:** First: blood cannot be manufactured, and Luxembourg's hospitals need about a hundred bags every weekday.
**ANNA:** Second: if you are healthy, at least 18 years old, and weigh more than 50 kilos, you can probably give.
**TOM:** Third: plan about one hour, and the donation itself takes only about ten minutes.
**ANNA:** And fourth: everything starts on dondusang dot l-u — the online test, the appointment, the places and the dates.
**TOM:** One hour of your time can help to save a life.
**ANNA:** And it's a beautiful way to help. That was our episode about blood donation in Luxembourg. **ANNA:** Thank you so much for listening.
