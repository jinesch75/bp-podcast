# Podcast Script — "I Can Vote – Your Voice in Your Commune"

**Part of the Biergerpakt programme**
**Hosts:** Anna (woman) and Tom (man)
**Length:** about 9 minutes — spoken slowly, in simple English

---

**ANNA:** Hello, and welcome! It's really nice to have you with us today.
**TOM:** Hello everyone!
**ANNA:** This podcast is part of the Biergerpakt. That's a programme of living together in Luxembourg, organized by the Ministry of Family Affairs, Solidarity, Living Together and Reception of Refugees. The idea is to help residents, and also cross-border workers, to discover Luxembourg — or maybe re-discover Luxembourg — and to meet new people.
**TOM:** I have a question to start with, Anna. Who decides whether there will be a new playground in your street, or a bus stop near your home?
**ANNA:** I would say my commune. The local council takes that kind of decision.
**TOM:** And who chooses the people who sit on that council?
**ANNA:** The residents do, at the municipal elections. And that is today's topic: the right to vote in Luxembourg when you are not a Luxembourger, and the website "I can vote".
**TOM:** The address in English is icanvote dot l-u. I can vote: the name says it all.
**ANNA:** The website is run by the Ministry of Family Affairs, Solidarity, Living Together and Reception of Refugees. It exists in five languages: French, German, English, Portuguese and Luxembourgish.
**TOM:** Let's begin with the basics. Which elections are there in Luxembourg?
**ANNA:** There are three main ones: the municipal elections, for the council of your commune, the European elections, for the European Parliament, and the national elections, for the Chamber of Deputies.
**TOM:** And can someone without Luxembourgish nationality vote in all three of them?
**ANNA:** Not in all three, because the national elections are only for people with Luxembourgish nationality. But the other two are open to many more people than you might think.
**TOM:** Let's take the municipal elections first. Who is allowed to vote there?
**ANNA:** Every adult who lives in Luxembourg, whatever their nationality. European or not European, it makes no difference.
**TOM:** Is that true? I thought you had to live here for several years first.
**ANNA:** That was the old rule: five years of residence. It was abolished a few years ago. Today, you can register as soon as you live here.
**TOM:** So what are the conditions today?
**ANNA:** There are three conditions: you are at least eighteen on election day, you live in Luxembourg, and you have not lost your right to vote, for example through a court decision.
**TOM:** And for people from outside the European Union and the Schengen area, is there anything more?
**ANNA:** Only one thing: a valid residence permit or residence card. People who are still applying for international protection cannot register yet. Once protection is granted, they can.
**TOM:** There is one important difference with Luxembourgers, though.
**ANNA:** Yes. Luxembourgers are put on the electoral list automatically when they turn eighteen. Everyone else has to register. Nobody will do it for you.
**TOM:** How does that registration work in practice?
**ANNA:** There are two ways, and the first one is online, on MyGuichet dot l-u, for example with your LuxTrust. It takes only a few minutes.
**TOM:** And what is the second way?
**ANNA:** You go to the population office of your commune with your identity card or your passport, and you sign a short declaration. If you need a residence permit to live here, you bring that too.
**TOM:** Is there a deadline for registering?
**ANNA:** You can register at any time of the year. But to vote in a particular election, you must register at the latest fifty-five days before election day.
**TOM:** Fifty-five days: that is almost two months before.
**ANNA:** Exactly, and that is why it is a good idea not to wait. If you register today, it's done, and it stays done.
**TOM:** What do you mean when you say it stays done?
**ANNA:** You register only once. Your name stays on the list for the following elections, and if you move to another commune, your registration moves with you automatically.
**TOM:** Now, there is something people should know before they register. In Luxembourg, voting is compulsory.
**ANNA:** That's right. Once you are on the list, voting is not only a right. It is also a duty, exactly as it is for Luxembourgers.
**TOM:** Does that obligation last forever?
**ANNA:** No. You can ask your commune to take your name off the list at any time. And people over seventy-five are excused.
**TOM:** And what happens if I am travelling on election day?
**ANNA:** Then you can vote by post. Every registered voter can ask for a postal vote, online on MyGuichet dot l-u or with a letter to the commune. Just ask early enough, because there are deadlines.
**TOM:** Could I simply ask a friend or my partner to vote for me?
**ANNA:** No, that is not allowed. In Luxembourg, nobody can vote in your place.
**TOM:** Many people have another worry. If I vote in my commune here, what about the elections in my country of origin?
**ANNA:** The municipal elections here are local: they concern the place where you live. For any question about elections in your country of origin, your embassy or your consulate is the right contact.
**TOM:** Let's talk about election day itself. How does it work?
**ANNA:** A few days before, your commune sends you a letter. It gives you the date, the polling station and the opening hours, and it includes the list of candidates.
**TOM:** And what do I do on the day itself?
**ANNA:** You go to your polling station, which is open from eight in the morning until two in the afternoon. You bring an identity document, you go into the booth, and you vote in secret.
**TOM:** How many votes do I have?
**ANNA:** As many as there are seats on your council. In small communes, you vote for individual people. In bigger communes, you vote for lists, and you can even mix candidates from different lists.
**TOM:** That sounds a little complicated.
**ANNA:** It is easier than it sounds. The website has a short video that shows how to fill in the ballot paper correctly.
**TOM:** Now the European elections. Who can vote in those?
**ANNA:** Citizens of the European Union who live in Luxembourg. They have a choice: vote here for Luxembourg's members of the European Parliament, or vote for the candidates of their country of origin.
**TOM:** But they cannot do both, I suppose.
**ANNA:** No. Each person votes only once, in one country.
**TOM:** And is the registration the same as for the municipal elections?
**ANNA:** It works in the same way, but it is a separate list. If you want to vote in both elections, you register for both.
**TOM:** When do the next elections take place?
**ANNA:** The next municipal elections and the next European elections are both planned for 2029. That may seem far away, but you can register right now.
**TOM:** Let me ask the question many listeners have in mind. Why should I vote? I am only one person.
**ANNA:** Think about what a commune decides. School buildings and childcare, local roads and cycle paths, water and waste, sport and culture, and the way your town or village is built.
**TOM:** So, these are things I see every day when I leave my house.
**ANNA:** Exactly. And the council is elected for six years. That means six years of decisions about the place where you live.
**TOM:** And in Luxembourg, almost half of the residents do not have Luxembourgish nationality.
**ANNA:** Yes, and in some communes they are the majority. When they don't vote, a very large part of the population has no voice in those decisions.
**TOM:** And so far, only a minority of them have registered.
**ANNA:** That is exactly why this campaign exists. Its slogan for the municipal elections is: "My voice, my choice, for the future of my municipality."
**TOM:** Can I do more than vote? Could I be a candidate myself?
**ANNA:** Yes, you can stand as a candidate if you are eighteen and have lived in your commune for at least six months. Residents without Luxembourgish nationality can become councillor, alderman, or even mayor.
**TOM:** And between two elections, is there a way to take part?
**ANNA:** There is. Communes have a commission for intercultural living together. It advises the local council, and residents can become members, whatever their nationality.
**TOM:** And what about cross-border workers, who live in another country?
**ANNA:** They cannot vote here, because voting depends on where you live. But they can be a member of that commission in the commune where they work.
**TOM:** And the meetings of the local council are public, aren't they?
**ANNA:** They are, and you can simply go and listen to what your council discusses.
**TOM:** Let's bring it together. What should people remember?
**ANNA:** First: if you live in Luxembourg and you are eighteen, you can vote in your commune, whatever your nationality, and however long you have been here.
**TOM:** Second: citizens of the European Union can also vote here in the European elections.
**ANNA:** Third: you have to register, online on MyGuichet dot l-u or at your commune, at the latest fifty-five days before the election. And once you are registered, voting is compulsory.
**TOM:** And fourth: you can do more. Be a candidate, join a commission in your commune, or simply go and listen to your council.
**ANNA:** You can find all the information on icanvote dot l-u.
**TOM:** You live here, so your voice counts here.
**ANNA:** And living together also means deciding together. That was our episode about "I can vote".
