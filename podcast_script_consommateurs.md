# Podcast Script — "Consumer Protection – The ULC and the European Consumer Centre"

**Part of the Biergerpakt programme**
**Hosts:** Anna (woman) and Tom (man)
**Length:** about 11 minutes — spoken slowly, in simple English

---

**ANNA:** Hello, and welcome! It's really nice to have you with us today.
**TOM:** Hello everyone!
**ANNA:** This podcast is part of the Biergerpakt. That's a programme of living together in Luxembourg, organized by the Ministry of Family Affairs, Solidarity, Living Together and Reception of Refugees. The idea is to help residents, and also cross-border workers, to discover Luxembourg — or maybe re-discover Luxembourg — and to meet new people.
**TOM:** Today's episode is about something that all of us did this week, and probably even today: we bought something.
**ANNA:** A coffee, a train ticket, a phone subscription, or a pair of shoes ordered online. Most of the time, everything goes well.
**TOM:** But sometimes the product is broken, the parcel never arrives, or the bill is much higher than expected. And then many people do not know who can help them.
**ANNA:** That is why we present two addresses today. The first one is the ULC, the consumer association of Luxembourg.
**TOM:** And the second one is the European Consumer Centre, which helps when the seller is in another European country.
**ANNA:** Let's begin with the ULC. What do these three letters stand for?
**TOM:** They come from the French name. In English, it is the Luxembourg Consumer Protection Association, and in French, Union luxembourgeoise des consommateurs.
**ANNA:** And what kind of organisation is it?
**TOM:** It is a non-profit association. Its mission has four parts: to protect, to defend, to inform and to educate consumers.
**ANNA:** Has this association existed for a long time?
**TOM:** Yes, it was founded in 1961. Today, tens of thousands of households in Luxembourg are members.
**ANNA:** Is the ULC part of the State, or is it independent?
**TOM:** It is independent, and that is important. Most of its money comes from the fees of its members, and it accepts no money from companies. The State also supports its work, on the basis of an agreement.
**ANNA:** So it can speak freely in the name of consumers.
**TOM:** Yes, and that is an important part of its work. The ULC represents consumers before public authorities, politicians and the courts, and it gives its opinion on new laws.
**ANNA:** And for each of us, it offers very concrete help. Even if you are not a member, you can call the ULC for a first telephone consultation, and it is free.
**TOM:** What can I expect from this first call?
**ANNA:** An adviser listens to your question about consumer law. A general question can often be answered directly on the phone.
**TOM:** And what if my case is more complicated, with a contract, a quote and several invoices?
**ANNA:** Then the adviser needs to read your documents. The analysis of a complete file is reserved for members, so you will be invited to join the association.
**TOM:** Some people find it hard to explain a problem on the phone. Is there another way for them?
**ANNA:** Yes, they can ask for a first free meeting with an adviser instead.
**TOM:** And how does the membership work?
**ANNA:** You pay a membership fee once a year. The membership covers you, your husband, wife or partner, and the children who live with you and have no income of their own.
**TOM:** And what do members receive in return?
**ANNA:** First of all, they receive personal help. Advisers and legal experts inform you and assist you in a dispute, for example by writing to the company.
**TOM:** Is that help only about the things that we buy?
**ANNA:** No, and many people do not know this. The ULC also helps its members with questions about renting a home, so about the contract between a tenant and a landlord.
**TOM:** I have heard that it also helps with building and renovation work.
**ANNA:** That is right: technical advisers can visit a building site or a home, to look at defects or at work that was badly done. Members can also get advice for their income tax return.
**TOM:** And what if a dispute really cannot be solved in a friendly way?
**ANNA:** Then the ULC can pay a part of the costs of a lawyer or of an expert. There are conditions and limits, and there is a waiting period after you join.
**TOM:** So it is better to become a member before the problem arrives.
**ANNA:** That is good advice for everyone. Members also receive the magazine of the ULC, and the results of its product tests.
**TOM:** There is also a lot of information on the website: brochures, model letters, and articles on themes such as guarantees, online shopping, travel, insurance or banking services. A part of it is open to everyone, and the rest is for members.
**ANNA:** Are there things that the ULC cannot do?
**TOM:** Yes, there are some limits. It deals with disputes between a consumer and a professional. It is not competent for a problem with a public administration.
**ANNA:** For that, there is the Ombudsman, and we have a whole episode about it.
**TOM:** And for problems with travel or with insurance, the ULC has built special solutions together with the professionals of these sectors.
**ANNA:** How do these two solutions work?
**TOM:** For package travel, there is the Luxembourg commission for travel disputes, which the ULC runs together with the travel agencies. For insurance, there is an insurance mediator, set up with the association of insurance companies.
**ANNA:** Do I have to pay for these two procedures?
**TOM:** No, both are free of charge. But first, you must complain in writing to the company yourself. Then they look for an agreement, and you remain free to accept or to refuse the solution that is proposed.
**ANNA:** Where can we find the ULC, and does it have a website?
**TOM:** Its office is in Howald, and consultations there are by appointment only. Advisers are also present in Ettelbruck at certain times. The website is ulc dot l-u, in French and in German.
**ANNA:** Now imagine that the seller is not in Luxembourg. You ordered a sofa from a website in Germany, or you rented a car in Spain.
**TOM:** That happens to many of us, in a small country with open borders. Who helps in that case?
**ANNA:** The European Consumer Centre Luxembourg helps you. Many people here simply say CEC, the three letters of its French name.
**TOM:** And what exactly is the role of this centre?
**ANNA:** It informs, advises and assists consumers who live in Luxembourg and who have a problem with a professional in another country of the European Union, in Iceland or in Norway.
**TOM:** Why these countries in particular?
**ANNA:** Because the centre belongs to a European network. There is a European Consumer Centre in every country of the European Union, and also in Iceland and in Norway, and the centres work together.
**TOM:** How much does this help cost?
**ANNA:** It costs nothing at all. The services are completely free of charge, and there is no membership.
**TOM:** Who pays for the centre, then?
**ANNA:** It is financed together by the European Commission, the Luxembourg State and the ULC. So our two addresses of today are partners.
**TOM:** Can you give us some typical examples?
**ANNA:** A flight is cancelled or arrives very late. A suitcase is lost or damaged. Something that you bought online is never delivered, or it arrives broken. Or you have a problem with a rental car abroad.
**TOM:** And here too, there is a clear order to follow. The first step is always to write to the company yourself, and to explain what happened and what you are asking for.
**ANNA:** Is that first step really necessary?
**TOM:** Yes, the centre can only act if you have first complained to the company. On its website, you find model letters and emails that help you to do this.
**ANNA:** And what if the company does not answer, or refuses?
**TOM:** Then you contact the European Consumer Centre. You describe the problem clearly, and you add copies of your documents: the contract, the invoice, the emails and the letters.
**ANNA:** How can I send all these documents?
**TOM:** There is an online complaint form on the website. You can also write an email or a letter, or call the centre. A visit to the office is possible, but only by appointment.
**ANNA:** What happens with my file afterwards?
**TOM:** Legal experts study it and tell you how to continue. If it is useful, the centre contacts the foreign company, to look for a solution that both sides can accept.
**ANNA:** Can the centre force the company to pay me back?
**TOM:** No, it has no power to force a company, and it cannot go to court for you. Its goal is always a friendly solution.
**ANNA:** And what if no friendly solution is found?
**TOM:** Then the centre explains the other possibilities. One of them is a mediation body in the country of the company. Another one is the European small claims procedure: a court procedure for smaller amounts, which is mainly done in writing and for which you do not need a lawyer.
**ANNA:** In which languages can I speak with the team?
**TOM:** In several languages, among them French, German, English and Luxembourgish. And the website exists in French, in English and in German, with a page in easy-to-read language.
**ANNA:** There are also some limits to know. The centre helps consumers, so private persons. It does not deal with a dispute between two companies, or between two private persons.
**TOM:** And what if the company is outside Europe, for example an online shop on another continent?
**ANNA:** Then the network cannot treat the case in the same way. It works for companies in the European Union, in Iceland and in Norway.
**TOM:** And what about our listeners who work in Luxembourg but live in France, Belgium or Germany: which centre is theirs?
**ANNA:** Each person contacts the European Consumer Centre of the country where he or she lives. So a cross-border worker turns to the centre of his or her country of residence.
**TOM:** The website of the centre is also full of practical advice. Two rules are especially useful to know.
**ANNA:** Which rule would you like to share first?
**TOM:** When you buy something online, by phone or by post from a professional, you normally have fourteen days to change your mind. This is a minimum right everywhere in the European Union.
**ANNA:** But this right does not exist when you buy in a shop or at a fair. And there are exceptions online too, for example for personalised products or for concert tickets.
**TOM:** The second rule is the legal guarantee. For a new product, it lasts two years in the whole European Union, even if the seller writes something else.
**ANNA:** You find all of this on the website of the centre, at cecluxembourg dot l-u.
**TOM:** Let's bring it all together. What should people remember?
**ANNA:** First: for a problem with a professional in Luxembourg, the ULC is your contact. A first telephone consultation is free, and members receive personal help.
**TOM:** Second: for a problem with a professional in another country of the European Union, in Iceland or in Norway, the European Consumer Centre helps you free of charge.
**ANNA:** Third: always write to the company first, and keep a copy of everything.
**TOM:** And fourth: both look for friendly solutions. They cannot force a company, but they know your rights and the next steps.
**ANNA:** You find them on ulc dot l-u and on cecluxembourg dot l-u.
**TOM:** A consumer who knows his or her rights buys with more confidence.
**ANNA:** And nobody has to stay alone with a problem like this. That was our episode about consumer protection, with the ULC and the European Consumer Centre. **ANNA:** Thank you so much for listening.
