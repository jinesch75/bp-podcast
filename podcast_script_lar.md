# Podcast Script — "Luxembourg Air Rescue – Rescue Helicopters and Repatriation"

**Part of the Biergerpakt programme**
**Hosts:** Anna (woman) and Tom (man)
**Length:** about 10 minutes — spoken slowly, in simple English

---

**ANNA:** Hello, and welcome! It's really nice to have you with us today.
**TOM:** Hello everyone!
**ANNA:** This podcast is part of the Biergerpakt. That's a programme of living together in Luxembourg, organized by the Ministry of Family Affairs, Solidarity, Living Together and Reception of Refugees. The idea is to help residents, and also cross-border workers, to discover Luxembourg — or maybe re-discover Luxembourg — and to meet new people.
**TOM:** Sometimes you hear it before you see it: the sound of a helicopter flying low over the roofs, or landing in a field next to a motorway. Many people in Luxembourg know that sound, and they know that somebody needs help very quickly.
**ANNA:** And very often, that helicopter belongs to Luxembourg Air Rescue, or LAR for short. Today we talk about this organisation: its rescue helicopters, its ambulance aircraft, and what it can do for you when you are ill or injured far from home.
**TOM:** LAR is a non-profit association. It was founded in 1988 by a group of firefighters, and today it flies rescue helicopters and ambulance aircraft, day and night, all year round, in Luxembourg, in the Greater Region and around the world.
**ANNA:** Is LAR a public service run by the State, or is it something different?
**TOM:** It is something different: LAR describes itself as a private non-profit organisation of public utility, which means that it works for the common good. It is not an insurance company: it relies on the fees of its members and on donations, and it has well over one hundred thousand members.
**ANNA:** Let's start with the helicopters: when there is a serious accident in Luxembourg, you do not call the helicopter yourself. You call the emergency number 112, and the emergency control centre decides whether the helicopter is the best help, depending on how serious the situation is, how easy the place is to reach, and which teams are available.
**TOM:** The emergency number and the fire and rescue services have their own episode in this podcast. Once the decision is made, how quickly does the helicopter take off?
**ANNA:** Very quickly: the crew is in the air about two minutes after the alert. The helicopter bases are on permanent standby, and LAR says that any place in Luxembourg can be reached in about ten minutes.
**TOM:** And who is on board when the helicopter lands at the scene of an accident?
**ANNA:** Next to the pilot, every helicopter carries an emergency doctor and an intensive care nurse from LAR. During the flight, the nurse also assists the pilot. So the treatment starts right where the accident happened.
**TOM:** What kind of emergencies does the helicopter fly to, most of the time?
**ANNA:** Mainly road accidents, accidents during leisure activities, and strokes. A seriously injured patient is flown to the hospital that is best equipped to treat them, and the medical team then hands the patient over to the hospital staff.
**TOM:** The helicopters also carry patients from one hospital to another, for example within the Greater Region, or from a hospital in Luxembourg to a specialist clinic abroad. And for many years, one helicopter has also flown emergency missions in two neighbouring German states, Saarland and Rhineland-Palatinate, under agreements with them.
**ANNA:** What happens when the place is very hard to reach, for example a cliff or a steep forest?
**TOM:** Then the helicopter can use a rescue hoist, a cable that lowers a rescuer and lifts the patient up. LAR trains for this together with the specialists of the fire and rescue services, and they have already worked together on real rescues on cliffs.
**ANNA:** Here is a question that many people ask: does the helicopter only come for members of LAR?
**TOM:** No, it does not: LAR is part of the emergency system in Luxembourg, so it also carries out rescue missions for people who are not members. The membership fees help to keep this service available for everyone.
**ANNA:** And if a helicopter lands close to where I am, what should I do?
**TOM:** The website gives some simple safety rules. Never walk towards the helicopter without permission from the crew, keep a safe distance, and stay where the pilot can see you. Crouch down when it comes close, and hold on to loose things such as scarves or umbrellas.
**ANNA:** That is good to know, and it brings us to the second big activity, bringing people home from abroad. Imagine that you fall seriously ill on holiday, or that you are badly injured during a business trip: what can LAR do then?
**TOM:** If you are a member, LAR can bring you home. It organises the whole journey, from the hospital bed abroad to a hospital bed at home or near your home. This is called a medical repatriation, and it is possible after holidays, studies and business trips.
**ANNA:** What is the very first thing to do in a situation like that?
**TOM:** Call the alert centre of LAR. Its number is on the membership card, and the centre is open day and night, every day of the year. The team asks where you are, what happened, and how to reach the doctor who is treating you.
**ANNA:** And who decides whether the person can travel, and in what way?
**TOM:** A doctor from LAR speaks directly with the doctor abroad. Together they look at the treatment on site and the risks of the journey, and then LAR's medical team decides whether, when and how the patient can travel safely.
**ANNA:** So the journey home does not always mean an ambulance plane?
**TOM:** No, not always: depending on the patient, it can be a road ambulance, a normal scheduled flight with or without a medical escort, a helicopter, or one of LAR's own ambulance jets, with a doctor and a nurse on board.
**ANNA:** And for a member, how much does all of this cost?
**TOM:** For members, LAR covers the costs of the repatriation from abroad, within the limits of its general conditions. And there are some conditions that are worth knowing before you travel.
**ANNA:** Then let's go through them together: which ones are the most important?
**TOM:** First, the cover starts one month after LAR receives the first membership fee. Second, a stay abroad must not last more than a hundred days in a row, except with the plan for young people. And third, a repatriation is not possible from countries at war or in serious unrest, when the safety of the patient and the crew cannot be guaranteed.
**ANNA:** Are there also medical situations that are not covered at all?
**TOM:** Yes, for example a minor illness or injury that can be treated well on the spot, complications after a planned operation abroad, or an illness that was already known before you left and was likely to need a repatriation. The full list is in the general conditions on the website.
**ANNA:** Now, how do you become a member? There are two main kinds of membership: an individual membership for one person, and a family membership. You join online on the website, and you pay a fee once a year; the amount is shown on the website.
**TOM:** Who exactly counts as family in a family membership?
**ANNA:** A couple who officially live at the same address, married or not, and the children of the household up to the age of eighteen. There is also a cheaper plan for young people between eighteen and twenty-five, and a membership for companies that want to protect their employees abroad.
**TOM:** Can cross-border workers become members too, or only people who live in Luxembourg?
**ANNA:** What counts is your main residence: you can join if you live in Luxembourg or in one of several European countries, among them France, Belgium and Germany. The full list of countries is on the website.
**TOM:** Do I need to carry my membership card with me all the time?
**ANNA:** No, you do not, because the alert centre can find you with your personal details. But here is one piece of advice from us: before you travel, save the number of the alert centre in your phone. And remember that the membership must be renewed every year.
**TOM:** Is the membership fee a kind of donation that I can deduct from my taxes?
**ANNA:** In principle, it is not, because the fee is not a donation. Donations go to the Luxembourg Air Rescue Foundation, which supports LAR with money and technical equipment, and a donation like that can be deducted.
**TOM:** Besides rescue and repatriation, LAR also helps in humanitarian crises, for example after earthquakes, together with the Ministry of Foreign and European Affairs. It describes itself as an official partner of the United Nations and of NATO. And its aircraft also carry organs for transplant centres in France.
**ANNA:** And for people who would like to learn first aid, is there something on offer as well?
**TOM:** Yes, there is the LAR Academy, a training centre at the airport in Findel. It offers first aid courses and other training for individuals, for groups and associations, and for companies and professionals. LAR also offers guided tours of its base, and it sometimes opens its doors to the public.
**ANNA:** You find all of this on the website, lar dot l-u, which exists in English, French and German. So, what should our listeners remember from this episode?
**TOM:** First, in an emergency in Luxembourg, call 112: the helicopter comes when it is the best help, whether you are a member or not. Second, when they are abroad, members call the alert centre of LAR, and an LAR doctor decides how to bring them home safely.
**ANNA:** And third, check your membership and its conditions before you travel. Because help from the sky is only really useful when you know how to ask for it. That was our episode about Luxembourg Air Rescue, the air rescue service with helicopters and ambulance aircraft. **ANNA:** Thank you so much for listening.
