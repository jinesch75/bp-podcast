# Podcast Script — "The Pensiounskeess: How Your Pension Works in Luxembourg"

**Part of the Biergerpakt programme**
**Hosts:** Anna (woman) and Tom (man)
**Length:** about 15 minutes — spoken slowly, in simple English

---

**ANNA:** Hello, and welcome! It's really nice to have you with us today.

**TOM:** Hello everyone!

**ANNA:** This podcast is part of the Biergerpakt. That's a programme of living together in Luxembourg, organized by the Ministry of Family Affairs, Solidarity, Living Together and Reception of Refugees. The idea is to help residents, and also cross-border workers, to discover Luxembourg — or maybe re-discover Luxembourg — and to meet new people.

**TOM:** Exactly. And today we talk about something that concerns every single person who works in Luxembourg. Retirement — and the pension you receive when you stop working.

**ANNA:** A big topic! And there is one office at the centre of it all. Tom, tell us its name.

**TOM:** In Luxembourgish, people call it the Pensiounskeess — the pension fund. Its official name is the Caisse nationale d'assurance pension. In English, the National Pension Insurance Office. The short name is C-N-A-P.

**ANNA:** The CNAP. And where can people find it online?

**TOM:** The website is c-n-a-p dot public dot l-u. It's available in French, German and English, and it explains the whole pension system in clear language.

**ANNA:** Perfect. So let's start simply. What is the CNAP?

**TOM:** The CNAP is the public office that pays the pensions of the general scheme. It exists in its current form since the first of January two thousand and nine, when four older pension funds were merged into one single fund.

**ANNA:** One fund for everyone?

**TOM:** For almost everyone. The general scheme covers all people working in Luxembourg — employees in the private sector and self-employed people. Civil servants and public employees have their own separate scheme.

**ANNA:** And cross-border workers?

**TOM:** Yes, absolutely. If you work in Luxembourg, you pay contributions in Luxembourg — it does not matter whether you live in the country or drive in every morning from France, Belgium or Germany. Your pension rights grow just the same.

**ANNA:** Good. And what exactly does the CNAP do?

**TOM:** Its mission is to grant pension benefits in three situations. Old age. Invalidity — when your health no longer lets you work. And survival — support for the family when an insured person dies.

**ANNA:** Old age, invalidity, survival. Before we look at each one — how is all this paid for?

**TOM:** Through contributions. Every month, a part of your salary goes to pension insurance. The cost is shared — the worker pays a part, the employer pays a part, and the State pays a part too.

**ANNA:** So while you work, you pay in. And later, the fund pays your pension.

**TOM:** Exactly. And every month you work in Luxembourg is written down in your personal insurance record. That record is the foundation of everything — your pension is calculated from it.

**ANNA:** We'll come back to that record — it sounds important. But first, the classic case. The old-age pension. When can I retire?

**TOM:** The legal retirement age in Luxembourg is sixty-five. To receive an old-age pension at sixty-five, you need at least ten years of insurance — one hundred and twenty months.

**ANNA:** Ten years. And do those ten years have to be in Luxembourg?

**TOM:** No — and this is very important for our international listeners. Insurance periods from other countries count towards the condition. If you worked five years in Luxembourg and twenty years in Belgium, you clearly pass the ten-year test.

**ANNA:** That's reassuring. And is the pension automatic? Does the money simply arrive on my sixty-fifth birthday?

**TOM:** No! This surprises many people. Even if you meet all the conditions, you must apply. You send a pension application to the CNAP — the forms are on the website.

**ANNA:** Good to know. Now, sixty-five is the legal age. But I've heard that some people retire earlier.

**TOM:** Yes. There is an early old-age pension, and it has two doors. The first door opens at fifty-seven — for people with a very long career of forty years of compulsory insurance. That's four hundred and eighty months of actual work.

**ANNA:** Forty years of work by age fifty-seven. So someone who started working very young.

**TOM:** Exactly. The second door opens at sixty. There you also need forty years, but more kinds of periods count towards them — for example, certain study years or child-raising periods, together with at least ten years of real contributions.

**ANNA:** And can I work while I receive an early pension?

**TOM:** Carefully! With the early pension, there are income limits. A small job is fine — up to one third of the minimum social wage, nothing changes. Above that, the pension can be reduced, or even withdrawn if you earn too much.

**ANNA:** And at sixty-five?

**TOM:** At sixty-five, total freedom. Once you receive the normal old-age pension, you can work as much as you like — it has no effect on your pension amount. And a nice detail — the pension contributions you pay on that new work can be reimbursed every year, on request.

**ANNA:** Really? The system truly rewards people who stay active. Okay — now let's open that insurance record you mentioned. What's inside?

**TOM:** Think of it as your pension diary. It lists, month by month, all your insurance periods. Luxembourg counts in calendar months — a month counts if you worked at least sixty-four hours as an employee, or ten days as a self-employed person.

**ANNA:** And how do I know what's written in my diary?

**TOM:** The CNAP sends you a career statement every year, automatically, if you were insured the year before. Check it! Make sure your months and salaries are correct. And from the age of fifty-five, the statement even shows an estimate of your future pension.

**ANNA:** An estimate — that really helps with planning. Now, you said some periods count even without contributions?

**TOM:** Yes — these are called additional periods. For example, periods of study or vocational training between eighteen and twenty-seven. Or the famous baby years — time spent raising young children under six.

**ANNA:** So a parent who stayed at home with the children is not forgotten.

**TOM:** Not at all. For two children, at least eight years of child-raising time can count; for three children, ten years. There are also periods for caring for a dependent person. These periods help you meet the conditions — they fill gaps in the career.

**ANNA:** That's fair. Now the question everyone is waiting for. How much pension will I get?

**TOM:** The exact calculation is complex, but the idea is simple. Your pension has two building blocks. The first is a flat-rate part — it depends on the length of your career, up to forty years. The second is a proportional part — it depends on the salaries you earned during your whole career.

**ANNA:** So — how long you worked, plus how much you earned.

**TOM:** Exactly. The longer the career and the higher the income, the higher the pension. And there is a bonus — if you work beyond a certain combination of age and career years, the rate of the proportional part goes up. Another encouragement to stay active.

**ANNA:** Is there a minimum? For people with small salaries?

**TOM:** Yes. With a full career of forty years, the law guarantees a minimum pension. In May twenty twenty-five, that minimum was about two thousand three hundred and fifty euros gross per month. If your calculated pension is below the minimum, you receive a supplement.

**ANNA:** And a maximum?

**TOM:** Yes, there is also a legal ceiling — no personal pension can go above it. And one more thing — pensions are not frozen. They follow the cost of living, with the index, and they are also adjusted to follow salaries. For example, on the first of January twenty twenty-six, pensions rose by one point five percent.

**ANNA:** So the pension keeps its value over time. And I've heard pensioners also get something extra in December?

**TOM:** Yes — the end-of-year allowance. People entitled to a pension on the first of December receive it. The amount depends on the career length — in twenty twenty-six, the maximum is a bit over one thousand euros for forty years of insurance.

**ANNA:** Very nice. Now — Luxembourg is a country of international careers. I work here, but before, I worked in Portugal, or Italy, or France. What happens to those years?

**TOM:** Nothing is lost — that's the golden rule. Inside the European Union, and also Switzerland, Iceland, Liechtenstein and Norway, all your insurance periods are added together to check the conditions. Luxembourg also has agreements with many other countries.

**ANNA:** Added together — so my Portuguese years help me qualify in Luxembourg.

**TOM:** Exactly. And then each country pays its own part — a partial pension for the years you worked there. You can receive several pensions at the same time, from several countries. In the end, you get one pension from each country where you built up rights.

**ANNA:** Do I have to apply in every country separately? That sounds like a lot of paperwork.

**TOM:** Good news — no. If you live in Luxembourg, you submit one application, to the CNAP. The CNAP then contacts the foreign pension institutions for you. It can take some time, because the offices exchange official documents, but it is one procedure.

**ANNA:** One application, and the offices talk to each other. Excellent. Now let's look at the second type of pension. Invalidity.

**TOM:** The invalidity pension is there when life takes a hard turn. If, because of a long illness or infirmity, you can no longer work in your profession or in another job that matches your strengths, you may receive an invalidity pension — before the age of sixty-five.

**ANNA:** What are the conditions?

**TOM:** In general, you need at least twelve months of insurance during the three years before the invalidity. But — important — if the invalidity comes from an accident or a recognised occupational disease, no waiting period is needed at all.

**ANNA:** And what happens when the person turns sixty-five?

**TOM:** The invalidity pension is automatically converted into an old-age pension. It's just a change of name — the amount is not recalculated. Nobody has to worry about that birthday.

**ANNA:** Good. And the third type — the survival pension.

**TOM:** Yes. When an insured person or a pensioner dies, the family is protected. The surviving husband or wife — and also the surviving registered partner — can receive a survival pension, calculated from the pension of the person who died.

**ANNA:** And the children?

**TOM:** Children receive an orphan pension, after the death of either the father or the mother. It is paid until the age of eighteen — and it can continue up to twenty-seven if the young person is still studying or in training for their future profession.

**ANNA:** That's an important safety net for families. Now, practical questions. How is the pension actually paid?

**TOM:** Every month, in advance, directly to your bank account. The CNAP publishes a payment calendar on its website, so you know the exact dates. Small example — the January pensions are usually paid just before Christmas.

**ANNA:** A nice present before the holidays! And how do I apply, step by step?

**TOM:** You download the right form from c-n-a-p dot public dot l-u — or ask for it. You fill it in, sign it, and send it by post with a bank statement showing your account. The CNAP sends you an acknowledgement, checks your career, and may ask for documents.

**ANNA:** And if there is a gap in my record — a year the CNAP doesn't know about?

**TOM:** They will ask you about it, and they can request official proof from other authorities, in Luxembourg and abroad. That's why it's smart to check your annual career statement long before retirement — problems are easier to fix early.

**ANNA:** Very good advice. And while we wait for the final decision — no money?

**TOM:** Don't worry. If the conditions are met, the CNAP normally pays you an advance on your pension while the final calculation is being completed. And many certificates — for example proof of your pension — can be requested online, including through MyGuichet dot l-u.

**ANNA:** Now Tom, we should mention the news. The pension system is changing a little, isn't it?

**TOM:** Yes. In December twenty twenty-five, the Chamber of Deputies adopted a reform of the pension system. The goal is to keep the system financially healthy for the long term — and the legal retirement age stays at sixty-five.

**ANNA:** What are the main changes?

**TOM:** The overall contribution rate rises from twenty-four percent to twenty-five point five percent. Conditions for some early pensions are adapted gradually, step by step, from July twenty twenty-six. Study years from the age of eighteen are considered more flexibly. And a progressive pension is introduced — a way to move into retirement gradually.

**ANNA:** And for people who are already retired?

**TOM:** Nothing changes. Pensions already being paid are not affected, and the old-age pension at sixty-five stays as it is. There is even a new tax advantage for people who could retire but choose to keep working — you can request it through MyGuichet dot l-u.

**ANNA:** So the message is — stay informed, but don't panic. For the details, always check the official pages.

**TOM:** Exactly. The CNAP website has a special news section about the twenty twenty-six changes, in several languages.

**ANNA:** And if someone has a personal question about their own file?

**TOM:** The CNAP has a pension hotline — the number is on the website. There is also a reception desk in Luxembourg City, open Monday to Friday, and contact forms online. And for cross-border careers, there are international information days, where specialists from Luxembourg, Germany, France and Belgium answer your questions together.

**ANNA:** That's a great service. Time for our little summary. Tom — the essentials?

**TOM:** The Pensiounskeess — the CNAP — pays the pensions of the private sector and the self-employed. Old-age pension at sixty-five, with at least ten years of insurance. Early pension possible at fifty-seven or sixty with a long career.

**ANNA:** The pension is never automatic — you must apply. Your insurance record is your pension diary — check your annual statement, and from fifty-five it shows an estimate.

**TOM:** Years worked in other countries are not lost — they are added together, and each country pays its part. One application at the CNAP is enough.

**ANNA:** Invalidity and survival pensions protect you and your family when life is difficult. And pensions follow the cost of living — plus a little extra at the end of the year.

**TOM:** And for everything — forms, the payment calendar, the reform news, the hotline — one address: c-n-a-p dot public dot l-u.

**ANNA:** That was our episode about the Pensiounskeess.
