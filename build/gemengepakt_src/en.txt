T: Last month, a small booklet arrived in my letterbox from my commune. It was in five languages, it listed the schools, the clubs and the local services, and the whole second half was about how I could get involved.
A: That sounds like a Biergerguide, a citizens' guide written for one commune. And it is a good way into today's topic: the communal pact for intercultural living together, called the Gemengepakt in Luxembourgish.
T: That is quite a long name, so let's start with the words: what does "intercultural living together" actually mean?
A: It comes from the law of 23 August 2023 on intercultural living together. The law describes it as a process that never really stops, in which everyone who lives or works in Luxembourg can take part and decide together, whatever their nationality.
T: And it rests on simple values: respect, tolerance, solidarity, and the fight against racism and discrimination. Diversity is seen as a strength. The law has been in force since the start of 2024.
A: And that is a real change of view: in the past, people talked about the integration of foreigners. Now the idea is that living together concerns everyone: Luxembourgers, newcomers, and the many people who cross the border every day to work here.
T: That is an important difference, and how does the State put all of this into practice?
A: The law gives the State four tools: a national action plan, the citizens' pact called the Biergerpakt, the Biergerpakt programme of modules and activities, and, as the fourth tool, the communal pact.
T: But why do communes need a pact of their own, when living together concerns the whole country?
A: Because living together happens first of all close to home: at school, in the clubs, in the street and at the town hall. The commune is where people meet every day, so the law gives communes a key role.
T: So who actually signs the communal pact, and is it only for big towns?
A: Three partners sign it: the commune, the ministry in charge of living together, which also runs the Biergerpakt, and the Syvicol, the association of Luxembourg's towns and communes. We have an episode about the Syvicol too. Communes of every size can sign, and several communes can also sign together, for example in a region or a nature park.
T: And the idea was tested first, in a pilot project from 2021 to 2023. Since the law came into force, more than fifty communes have signed the new pact.
A: That is a good start, and what does a commune commit to when it signs?
T: To a process over several years, with three main aims: access to information, the participation of everyone who lives or works in the commune, and the fight against racism and all forms of discrimination. A pact lasts at most six years, the length of a communal mandate.
A: Six years is a long time, so how is the work organised in practice?
T: It is done in cycles, each one on a theme, and each cycle has five steps. First, the political commitment: the commune's leaders decide to take part. Second, a look at the current situation: what already works well, and what is missing.
A: Third, citizen workshops, where residents and people who work in the commune come up with concrete actions together. Fourth, the commune puts those actions into practice. And fifth, everyone looks back and evaluates the work, before the next cycle starts.
T: Citizen workshops sound interesting: does that mean ordinary residents really have a say?
A: Yes, and that is the whole point: the actions are not invented in an office, they come from the people who live and work in the commune, and that includes cross-border workers.
T: So if my commune announces a citizen workshop, can I simply go along, even if I do not speak Luxembourgish?
A: Yes, you can: the commune must make sure that everyone who lives or works there can take part, and that its information is easy to understand. Some communes use translation for exactly that reason, so watch your commune's website and newsletter.
T: And who follows the pact inside the commune, to make sure that things really happen?
A: Every commune with a pact has a steering committee of at least five people: a member of the communal council, two members of the commune's committee on living together, two people from local associations, and the pact coordinator, if there is one.
T: You mentioned a coordinator, so who is that, and is it always someone from the town hall?
A: The coordinator supports the commune and follows the pact from day to day. It can be someone who works for the commune, or an outside person or organisation, and several communes can share one. The State helps to pay for the coordinator and for the costs of carrying out the pact.
T: And the communes are not left on their own. The ministry has advisers for intercultural living together, who give free advice to the communes with a pact and pass on good ideas from one commune to the next. Two partner organisations help as well: the ASTI and the CEFIS.
A: You talked about the commune's committee on living together a moment ago: what is that exactly?
T: Every commune in Luxembourg must have one, with or without a pact. It is an advisory committee, set up after each communal election, and it took over from the former integration committees. Its recommended name is the commission for intercultural living together.
A: And can anyone become a member, or is it only for Luxembourgers?
T: It is open to people who live or work in the commune: Luxembourgers, residents of other nationalities, and cross-border workers too. The communal council appoints the members, and at least one councillor sits on it. Many communes publish a call for candidates, so keep an eye on your commune's news.
A: That is good to know, and what does such a committee actually do during the year?
T: It looks at what helps or blocks living together locally, and it helps the commune to plan measures and activities. It encourages access to information, participation, local associations and dialogue between residents, and it acts against racism and discrimination.
A: And it has one more task: it elects the commune's representatives to a national council for intercultural living together. That council advises the minister, so local experience also reaches the national level.
T: Now, back to the booklet in my letterbox: is the Biergerguide part of the pact?
A: It goes hand in hand with it. The ministry prepares it together with the Syvicol and the commune, in five languages. The first part is called "I discover my commune": the town hall, the schools, culture, clubs and activities. The second part, "I get involved in my commune", shows ways to take part, such as volunteering, learning a language, or politics.
T: Many communes have already received their own guide. So if your commune has one, it is worth keeping it close at hand.
A: And where does the Biergerpakt itself come in for the communes?
T: A communal pact also encourages residents, and cross-border workers who work in the commune, to join the Biergerpakt and to follow its modules. And every year, the commune receives a small amount from the State for each Biergerpakt member who lives or works there. So when you join, your commune benefits too.
A: That is a nice extra reason to sign up, and what kind of actions do communes actually come up with?
T: The official website collects good practices in five themes. For welcome and orientation, there are welcome days for newcomers, tours of the villages, and a public writer, who helps people to understand and write official letters.
A: For languages, there are language cafés, where people practise together, and QR codes on documents that lead to translations. For participation, some communes have a participatory budget, where residents propose projects and vote on them, and some translate the broadcasts of council meetings.
T: For meeting each other, there are neighbours' days, community gardens, and meeting places run by volunteers. And against discrimination, there is the living library, where you borrow a person instead of a book, for a conversation that breaks down prejudices.
A: I love that idea, and how do the communes learn from one another?
T: Through a forum on living together in our communes: it meets once a year at national level and several times a year in the regions, organised by the ministry with the ASTI, and supported by the CEFIS and the Syvicol.
A: And what happens when a pact comes to an end after those six years?
T: Six months before the end, the minister evaluates the pact and sends a report to the commune. To go on, the new communal council can apply again. And a commune that signs a pact receives the label "commune of intercultural living together".
A: So how can I find out whether my own commune has signed, and what it is doing?
T: The website gemengen dot zesummeliewen dot l-u has a map of the communes and all the good practices, in English, French and German. And your town hall can tell you who sits on the committee on living together. So, to sum up: the communal pact is a promise between your commune, the ministry and the Syvicol to make living together work locally. It runs in cycles, with citizen workshops at their heart, and everyone who lives or works in the commune can take part.
C: So join a workshop, ask about the committee on living together, and if you want to vote in the communal elections, listen to our episode "I Can Vote". That was our episode about the communal pact for intercultural living together.
