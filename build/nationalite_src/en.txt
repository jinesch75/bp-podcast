T: Two passports in one pocket: for many people in Luxembourg, that is completely normal. They have become Luxembourgish, and they have kept the nationality they had before.
A: And that brings us to today's topic: how do you get Luxembourgish nationality? Many people live here for years, work here, and see their children grow up here, so sooner or later the question comes up.
T: Let's start with the reason, then: what actually changes when you become Luxembourgish?
A: You get all the civil and political rights of a Luxembourger. For example, you can vote in the national elections, which are only open to Luxembourgish citizens. We explained the different elections in our episode "I Can Vote".
T: And you can keep your first nationality, as in my example, because Luxembourg does not ask you to give it up. But your other country has its own rules, and some countries take their nationality away when you choose a new one.
A: So the official advice is clear: before you start, ask the embassy or the consulate of your country whether you can keep its nationality. That way, you avoid a bad surprise.
T: That is good advice. And who takes care of nationality in Luxembourg, and where do I find reliable information?
A: The Nationality Office of the Ministry of Justice deals with the files. And the best place to start is Guichet.lu, the State's information portal. It explains every situation, and it even has a small chart that helps you find the path that fits you.
T: You say "the path", so I suppose there is more than one way to become Luxembourgish?
A: There are several: some people are Luxembourgish by law, without asking for anything. Others become Luxembourgish because they decide to, and for them there are three procedures: naturalisation, the option, and reclaiming nationality. Let's take them one by one.
T: I will start with nationality by law. A child is Luxembourgish from birth if one parent is Luxembourgish, and a child born in Luxembourg is also Luxembourgish if one parent was born here too. And when a parent becomes Luxembourgish, the children under eighteen become Luxembourgish as well.
A: And what about a child who is born in Luxembourg, but whose parents both have another nationality and were born abroad?
T: That child becomes Luxembourgish automatically at eighteen, if they have lived here legally for the five years just before their eighteenth birthday. For most young people today, there is one more condition: one parent must have lived here legally for the twelve months before the birth.
A: So the law already does a lot, and for adults who want to take the step themselves, the general path is naturalisation. It is open to every adult who has lived legally in Luxembourg for at least five years, and the last year must be without interruption.
T: Five years is not that long, but I suppose there are other conditions too?
A: There are three: you need to pass the Luxembourgish language test, and you need a certificate for a course called "Living together in the Grand Duchy of Luxembourg". And you must be of good repute: nationality is refused, for example, after certain serious criminal convictions, or if someone gives false information.
T: Let's take the language test first, because many people are worried about it, so what does it look like?
A: It tests speaking and listening, not writing. For speaking, you talk with an examiner and describe a picture, at a basic level called A2. For listening, you hear recordings, like a radio news item or an everyday conversation, and answer multiple-choice questions, at the next level, B1.
T: And a good listening result can make up for a weaker speaking result, which is reassuring. You register online with the National Institute for Languages. Then comes the course: it lasts twenty-four hours, and it covers the rights of citizens, the institutions of the State and the communes, and the history of Luxembourg and European integration.
A: Can you skip the course if you already know a lot about Luxembourg?
T: Yes, you can take an exam on the same topics instead. Registration is free and online, for the course and for the exam. And if a person's health does not allow them to learn these subjects or the language, they can ask the Minister of Justice for an exemption, with a certificate from a specialist doctor.
A: And here I see a link with our own programme. The Biergerpakt has a module of at least six hours that gives an overview of Luxembourg. With that certificate, you do not need to follow the history part of the course, and this is recognised automatically.
T: That is a nice extra reason to join the Biergerpakt. And what about money: do people have to pay for all this?
A: The naturalisation procedure itself is free. The language test has a registration fee, but you can ask the Ministry of Justice to refund it, under certain conditions, once you have handed in your nationality application. Some documents, like copies from other countries, can still cost a little.
T: And for people who work, there is help with time, too. Employees can ask for a special language leave to learn Luxembourgish: up to two hundred hours over a whole career, in two parts. It does not count against their normal holidays, and they do not need to live in Luxembourg to use it.
A: So, imagine I have my language certificate and my course certificate. What is the next step?
T: You go in person to the civil registrar of the commune where you live. You bring your birth certificate, a valid passport, a signed form about your life called the biographical questionnaire, and criminal record certificates from your other countries. You sign the application yourself, but you can bring someone along.
A: And what happens if a document is missing, or if it is written in a language that nobody at the commune can read?
T: The registrar asks for the missing documents, and you have three months to bring them. Documents that are not in French, German, English or Luxembourgish need a translation by a sworn translator.
A: Then the file goes to the Nationality Office, which checks it, and the Minister of Justice decides. The answer should come within eight months, usually by post. If it is yes, you are Luxembourgish from the date of that decision.
T: Eight months, so it is worth preparing well. You also mentioned the option: what is the difference with naturalisation?
A: The option is a simpler procedure for people who already have a special link with Luxembourg, and the law lists ten cases. If the Minister has no objection, you are Luxembourgish four months after the Ministry receives your file.
T: Ten cases is a lot, so let's look at the ones that concern the most people.
A: One case is for adults with a parent or grandparent who is, or was, Luxembourgish: they need no language test and no course. Another case is for people married to a Luxembourger. They do need the test and the course, and if they live abroad, they need three years of marriage.
T: There is also a case for people who have lived here legally for at least twenty years. They do not take the language test: they follow a Luxembourgish course of twenty-four hours, and that is all. And adults who went to school in Luxembourg for at least seven years need no test and no course.
A: That sounds fair, and what about people who came to Luxembourg as children or teenagers?
T: Adults who settled here before the age of eighteen have their own case, with five years of legal residence, the test and the course. And children born in Luxembourg can opt for nationality from the age of twelve, together with their parents, under conditions similar to the ones at eighteen.
A: That already covers many families, so who are the remaining cases for?
T: They are for parents of a Luxembourgish child, for refugees and stateless people, for volunteer soldiers in the Luxembourg army, and for people who have completed the introductory modules of the Biergerpakt. Most of them also need five years of legal residence, the test and the course.
A: So the Biergerpakt counts here too, and how does the option work in practice?
T: Just like naturalisation, you go in person to the civil registrar of your commune, and if you live abroad, you go to the City of Luxembourg. And the option is free of charge as well.
A: The third procedure is reclaiming nationality. It is for adults who were Luxembourgish and lost their nationality, and for women who lost it in the past because of their marriage. There was also a way for descendants of Luxembourgers from the year 1900, but its deadlines have now passed.
T: Let's bring it all together. First, ask your other country whether you can keep its nationality. Second, find your path on Guichet.lu: by law, naturalisation, the option or reclaiming. Third, for most adults, the steps are five years here, the language test and the course, and then an application at your commune, free of charge.
C: And the Biergerpakt can help you on the way, with its modules about life in Luxembourg and its language offers. That was our episode about getting Luxembourgish nationality.
